Research

Our laboratory studies why cancers stop responding to treatment, and what can be done about it. We approach this question by moving between molecular mechanism and disease context: dissecting the pathways that tumour cells use to survive therapy, and testing those insights in cell and animal systems that reflect the biology of real tumours. Our aim is to turn mechanistic understanding into rational strategies for tumours that current therapies fail.

Understanding and overcoming therapeutic resistance

Many cancers respond to targeted therapy at first, then adapt. We investigate how that adaptation happens at the molecular level, with particular attention to liver cancer, where the survival benefit of existing systemic therapy remains modest. A major focus is post-transcriptional regulation. Small non-coding RNAs act as switches that can reset the balance between growth, invasion and cell death, and we study how their loss or gain reshapes tumour behaviour under drug pressure — including their movement between cells as a means of spreading a resistant phenotype. Alongside this, we examine how cell-cycle regulators and stress-response proteins modulate sensitivity to treatment, on the premise that resistance is rarely the work of a single gene and is better understood as a shift in the network as a whole.

Cancer metabolism and the tumour–immune interface

Resistance is not only a signalling problem; it is also a metabolic one. Tumour cells reorganise how they generate and spend energy, and this reorganisation has consequences well beyond growth rate. We study how metabolic rewiring under therapeutic pressure alters the way tumour cells present themselves to the immune system, including the regulation of immune-checkpoint molecules. Framing metabolism as a mechanism of resistance rather than a by-product of it opens a second set of therapeutic entry points, and connects our work to functional imaging approaches that read metabolic state directly.

Endocrine and neuroendocrine tumour biology

Our third line of work concerns endocrine and neuroendocrine tumours — a group of relatively rare cancers for which effective systemic options remain limited and preclinical models are scarce. Using genetically defined animal models that develop these tumours spontaneously, we study how disruption of cell-cycle control initiates tumour formation, how growth-factor and survival signalling sustain it, and how pharmacological inhibition of these pathways affects tumour progression. Because the models recapitulate features of the corresponding human disease, they also serve as a testing ground for therapeutic and diagnostic strategies before clinical translation.
